There’s a defect in metal 3D printing that nobody talks about enough. It doesn’t make headlines like delamination failures or visible warping. It forms silently, on the surface of your part, every single time you press print — regardless of how carefully you’ve tuned your laser power or scan speed.

It’s called balling.

And in a recent study published in Materials (MDPI, 2022), my colleagues and I discovered something that the standard LPBF optimization playbook doesn’t account for: the geometry of your lattice structure and the angle of your laser scan path are independently driving how bad this problem gets — completely separate from the energy density parameters most engineers focus on.

If you’re working with metal additive manufacturing for aerospace, medical devices, or any structural application, this matters more than you might expect.

What is balling, and why should you care?

Imagine you’re laying a bead of solder across a surface. Under the right conditions, it flows out flat and bonds cleanly. Under the wrong conditions — wrong temperature, contaminated surface, wrong speed — it beads up into small spheres instead of spreading. You get little balls instead of a continuous track.

A schematic showing Balling phenomenon in LBPF fabricated samples (courtesy: Gemini)

LPBF does the same thing with metal powder. The laser melts a small pool of powder, and that pool is supposed to wet and fuse to the layer beneath it, building up solid material. When something goes wrong — too much surface tension, oxygen contamination, an unfavorable thermal environment — the melt pool doesn’t spread. Instead, it contracts into spherical balls.

These balls then sit on the surface of your part. They’re poorly bonded. They can obstruct the recoater blade on the next pass. They create stress concentration points. They reduce surface quality. In a porous lattice structure where you’re already working with thin struts and designed void space, they can be especially damaging — blocking pores, altering effective density, and creating initiation sites for cracks.

Most current guidance on avoiding balling focuses on laser process parameters: power, scan speed, and the resulting energy density delivered to the powder bed. Our study found that this picture is incomplete.

What we actually did — and what we found

We printed Schwartz Primitive TPMS lattice layers from NiTi shape memory alloy powder using an EOS M400 metal printer. We kept laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, and layer thickness constant throughout — and varied two things independently:

1. Relative density: samples ranging from 30% to 60% solid volume fraction 2. Scan strategy: four different approaches — parallel with 90° interlayer rotation, parallel with 0° rotation, inclined at 45°, and spiral.

The idea was to isolate the effect of structural design and scan direction from the laser energy variables that dominate most published optimization work.

What we found was clear enough to make you rethink your design workflow.

Finding 1: Denser lattices produce larger balling defects

This one surprised us. You might expect that a denser structure — more solid material, more connected struts — would give the laser more stable material to melt into and produce cleaner results. The opposite was true.

As relative density increased from 30% to 60%, the average size of balling defects grew. At 60% density, balls in the 50–80 µm range were significantly more prevalent than in the 30% density samples, which showed predominantly smaller balls in the 20–50 µm range.

The likely explanation relates to heat balance. In a porous structure, the surrounding void space and the thin struts create a specific thermal environment around each melt pool. As density increases, the local thermal mass changes — more solid material surrounds each melt event, altering how heat flows away from the melt pool and how quickly it solidifies. The result is a melt pool dynamic that favors the formation of larger, more prominent balling imperfections.

There was also a non-monotonic trend in cracking. Samples at 40–50% relative density showed less cracking than those at either extreme (30% or 60%). This suggests there’s a density window — at least for this alloy and these parameters — where the thermal gradients during solidification are more manageable. This kind of non-obvious optimum is exactly why empirical characterization matters, and why assuming a monotonic relationship between density and defect formation can lead you astray.

Finding 2: A 45° scan angle makes balling significantly worse

Of the four scan strategies tested, the inclined 45° strategy produced the most pronounced balling — noticeably worse than the parallel strategies with either 0° or 90° interlayer rotation.

Why? Scan angle affects how heat builds up and dissipates across the powder bed. When the laser traces diagonal paths across a square lattice geometry, the relationship between scan vector length, melt pool geometry, and the local thermal boundary conditions created by the lattice architecture changes in ways that promote balling. The inclined strategy essentially creates a mismatch between the scan path geometry and the structural geometry of the lattice — and the melt pool pays for it.

The parallel strategy with 90° interlayer rotation performed best overall. This is consistent with other literature showing that rotating the scan direction between layers promotes more uniform temperature distribution across the build, reducing residual stress gradients and giving the melt pool a more stable thermal environment to solidify within.

Crucially, average ball sizes across all scan strategies were in the 40–60 µm range — confirming that once balling occurs, the scan strategy influences its severity but not its fundamental presence. You can choose parameters that make it worse; avoiding it entirely requires a different approach.

The underlying physics in plain language

Both findings — density effect and scan angle effect — trace back to the same root cause: the thermal history of the melt pool is controlled not just by your laser settings, but by the local structural environment the laser is operating in.

In a solid block of metal, the thermal environment is relatively uniform and predictable. Each melt pool is surrounded by similar material on all sides, and the heat extraction geometry is consistent throughout the build. Standard parameter optimization works well in this regime.

In a porous TPMS lattice, this assumption breaks down. Each point in the structure has a different relationship to its surrounding geometry — some regions of a thin strut are near void space, others are near junctions with adjacent struts, others are near the build plate. The laser deposits the same energy at each point, but the thermal response is different everywhere. Scan angle then determines which parts of the geometry the laser interacts with first and in what sequence, adding another layer of complexity.

This is why you can optimize your LPBF parameters on dense coupons, transfer those parameters to a lattice design, and still get significant defects — the parameters that worked in the coupon were optimized for a thermal environment that no longer exists in the lattice.

What this means for engineers and designers

Design for printability, not just for performance. When you select a TPMS topology and relative density for an application — whether it’s a bone implant, a heat exchanger insert, or an impact-absorbing structure — that choice is also a choice about printability. Higher densities may give you better mechanical performance on paper but worse as-printed quality. There’s a tradeoff that needs to be quantified, not assumed away.

Don’t default to 45° scan strategies for complex lattice geometries. The 45° inclined strategy is often recommended for residual stress reduction in solid or near-solid parts. Our results suggest this recommendation doesn’t transfer cleanly to porous architected structures — at least not for NiTi TPMS. Test it explicitly rather than inheriting it from dense-part best practices.

Include geometry as a variable in your process qualification. Current process qualification approaches for AM typically involve printing test coupons and characterizing material properties. For lattice structures, this is insufficient. The geometry itself is a process variable. Qualification needs to include the specific topology and density range of the intended application.

Balling is not eliminated by parameter optimization alone. Our results, combined with the broader literature, suggest that balling in LPBF is a persistent feature of the process rather than a defect that can be fully optimized away for complex geometries. The practical goal is managing its severity and understanding its distribution — not assuming it can be eliminated by tweaking energy density alone.

The bigger picture: What’s missing from how we talk about AM quality

There’s a tendency in additive manufacturing — especially in commercial contexts — to describe LPBF as a mature, well-controlled process where the main remaining challenge is material qualification and post-processing. For simple geometries and well-characterized alloys, there’s some truth to that.

For complex architected structures in shape memory alloys, we’re not there yet. Not even close.

The interactions between geometry, process parameters, material behavior, and thermal history in LPBF of NiTi TPMS lattices are still being mapped. Each paper in this space — including this one — adds a piece of that map. The finding that scan strategy and relative density independently drive defect formation in ways that existing process windows don’t predict is exactly the kind of result that needs to be built into design tools, process standards, and quality assurance frameworks.

Until it is, engineers working with these structures need to approach process selection empirically, characterize their as-printed quality rather than assuming it, and resist the temptation to transfer process parameters from one structural context to another without validation.

A closing thought

When I started this work, I expected process parameters to dominate the results. Laser power, energy density, scan speed — these are the levers the AM community has spent the most time studying, and they’re the first things engineers reach for when something goes wrong.

What the data showed was more interesting: the shape of your part and the angle of your laser path are competing with those parameters for control over what actually happens in the melt pool. For a class of structures as geometrically distinctive as TPMS lattices, that competition matters enormously.

It’s a reminder that in materials engineering, the variables you don’t control are often as important as the ones you do.
