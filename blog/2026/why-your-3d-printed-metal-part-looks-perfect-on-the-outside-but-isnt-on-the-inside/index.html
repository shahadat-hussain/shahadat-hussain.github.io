There’s a moment every materials researcher dreads: you’ve spent weeks optimizing your laser parameters, printed a beautiful lattice structure, and when you finally cut it open and look inside under the electron microscope — it’s chaos.

Not catastrophic failure. Not obvious defects. Just... inconsistency. Fine grains here. Coarse grains there. Columnar growth in one region, equiaxed grains in the next. A microstructural fingerprint so complex it almost looks random.

That moment happened to me while working on one of the more exciting challenges in advanced manufacturing: 3D printing NiTi (Nickel-Titanium) shape memory alloys into triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) lattice structures. And what we found changes how I think about the reliability of metal additive manufacturing — and should change how industry evaluates it too.

First, let me tell you why this material combination matters

NiTi shape memory alloys are remarkable. They’re the material behind self-expanding heart stents, orthodontic wires that apply constant gentle pressure, and experimental morphing aerospace components. When you cool them down and deform them, they snap back to their original shape when heated. When processed correctly, they can also be superelastic — recovering strains far beyond what ordinary steel can handle without any heat needed at all.

TPMS structures — Triply Periodic Minimal Surface lattices — are a class of architected geometries with mathematically smooth, curved surfaces that repeat in three dimensions. Think of them as nature-inspired scaffolding: the Gyroid and Schwarz Primitive topologies you might have seen in biology textbooks or engineering papers. These structures are prized for their exceptional surface-area-to-volume ratios, mechanical efficiency, and permeability — making them candidates for bone implants, heat exchangers, and impact-absorbing crash structures.

Put the two together — NiTi + TPMS — and you theoretically have a smart, porous, biocompatible structure that could change a patient’s implant, absorb impact, or morph on demand. The promise is enormous.

The challenge? Actually printing it.

The problem with printing shapes that have never existed before

Conventional manufacturing routes — machining, casting, forging — struggle badly with NiTi. The alloy’s superelasticity, which makes it so useful in service, makes it a nightmare to machine. It springs back, work-hardens unpredictably, and resists welding. You essentially can’t make a porous NiTi scaffold through traditional means at any reasonable complexity.

Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) changes that equation. It builds parts layer by layer from metal powder using a focused laser beam, enabling geometries that were simply impossible before. For porous, complex structures like TPMS lattices, LPBF isn’t just convenient — it’s the only practical route.

But LPBF introduces its own problem: the laser creates, melts, and resolidifies material millions of times across a build. Each tiny melt pool heats and cools at extreme rates. And when your geometry is complex — when you have curved overhanging surfaces, thin struts, and varying local mass distributions — the thermal history of each point in the part becomes wildly different from every other point.

That’s what we set out to investigate.

What we found when we looked inside

We printed two TPMS geometries — the Schwarz Primitive and the Schoen Gyroid — from pre-alloyed NiTi powder using an EOS M400-4 metal printer at Khalifa University. Same laser parameters for both. Same energy density of 74.1 J/mm³. Same scanning strategy.

When we sectioned the parts and examined them under a scanning electron microscope, the results were striking.

Scanning Electron Microscope Images of LPBF manufactured NiTi Gyroid Lattice showing inhomogeneous microstructural features in a single sample

Both structures showed severe microstructural inhomogeneity. In a single micrograph spanning just 30 micrometres, we could identify up to ten distinct microstructural regions: very fine grains, equiaxed grains, elongated grains, columnar growth, dendritic structures, and transitional zones between them — all within the same printed layer.

This matters because microstructure dictates mechanical properties. A region of fine grains will behave differently under load than a region of large columnar grains. A part that looks geometrically perfect on the outside may have property gradients running through it that no post-print inspection can easily detect.

There was also a geometry effect. The Gyroid, with its more complex curved geometry and nearly double the surface area per unit cell compared to the Primitive, showed significantly more columnar grain growth. The geometry itself — not just the laser settings — was influencing how the melt pool solidified. The shape of the structure was writing its thermal history, and that history was being preserved in the microstructure.

We also observed macro-scale defects driven by the same thermal instability: balling (spherical metal droplets formed by surface tension), porosity, cracking, and uneven surface formation. At the laser parameters tested, warping also limited the build height to just 1–1.5 mm before the printer’s recoater blade was obstructed.

What this means if you’re building products

If you’re an engineer or product developer working with metal additive manufacturing, here’s what I’d draw from this work:

1. Geometry is a process parameter. When you design a complex architected structure for LPBF, you’re not just specifying a shape — you’re specifying a thermal boundary condition that will influence every melt pool in the build. Thin walls, large overhangs, and varying cross-sections all create local thermal environments that can produce dramatically different microstructures. This needs to be designed for, not discovered after the fact.

2. Same process parameters ≠ same material properties. The common assumption that dialling in your laser power, speed, and hatch spacing will give you consistent material is only true for simple, solid geometries. Once you introduce architectural complexity, those parameters interact with geometry in ways that produce inhomogeneous results. Quality assurance strategies need to account for this.

3. Inspection strategies must evolve. Surface finish inspection and even CT scanning have limits when it comes to detecting microstructural gradients. For safety-critical applications — medical implants, aerospace structures, load-bearing components — the field needs better process monitoring and in-situ quality assurance tools. Real-time thermal imaging during the build, as pioneered by other researchers, is one promising direction.

4. The potential remains enormous — but so does the engineering challenge. Nothing in this work diminishes the promise of NiTi TPMS structures. It clarifies the engineering challenge. Inhomogeneous microstructures are not unique to NiTi — they’re a fundamental characteristic of LPBF-built metals. Understanding them is the first step to controlling them.

Where I see this going

The next frontier in this space isn’t just printing these structures — it’s printing them with controlled microstructure. That means:

  • Geometry-aware process planning: slicing software that adjusts laser parameters region by region based on local geometry

  • Real-time feedback control: using thermal sensors and machine learning to detect and correct thermal anomalies during the build

  • Post-process heat treatment optimization: tailored annealing cycles that homogenize microstructure without destroying the designed geometry

  • Computational microstructure prediction: models that predict grain structure from first principles, enabling virtual qualification before a single gram of powder is consumed

This is the intersection of computational design, advanced manufacturing, and materials characterization where the most impactful work will happen over the next decade — and where academic research needs to connect directly with industrial application.
