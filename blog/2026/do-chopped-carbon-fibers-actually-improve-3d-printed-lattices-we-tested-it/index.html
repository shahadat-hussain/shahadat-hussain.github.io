If you’ve spent any time reading about 3D printing and architected materials recently, you’ve probably seen the hype around Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS) structures.

Because of their zero-mean-curvature geometry, structures like the Gyroid don’t have the sharp nodes or corners found in standard strut lattices. This lets them distribute stress evenly and absorb huge amounts of energy without collapsing prematurely.

Naturally, the next step engineers take to push performance further is to throw chopped carbon fibers into the filament. Higher stiffness, higher strength — problem solved, right?

Not quite.

In practice, the literature on chopped fiber TPMS lattices has been surprisingly mixed. In some tests, short carbon fibers make parts stiffer; in others, the added brittleness actually makes the printed lattice perform worse than its unreinforced polymer counterpart under heavy compression.

To figure out what’s actually going on, our team at NYU Abu Dhabi put together a systematic study looking at the intersection of material, density, and post-processing.

Here is what we found.

The Experiment: 5 Materials, 3 Densities, and Heat Treatment

We 3D-printed Gyroid lattices across relative densities ranging from 20% to 40% using five distinct material systems:

  • VeroWhite (PolyJet)
  • Nylon 12 (Selective Laser Sintering)
  • ABS (Fused Filament Fabrication / FFF)
  • PLA-CF (10% carbon fiber by volume)
  • PPA-CF (Polyphthalamide with 15% carbon fiber by volume)

We also took a subset of the PPA-CF lattices and subjected them to a post-print thermal annealing process (80°C for 8–12 hours) to see if heat-treating could unlock latent mechanical performance.

Key Takeaway 1: Reinforcement Needs Density

At low relative densities (20%), material choice barely made a difference. The architecture itself dominates the behavior, meaning thin walls flex and deform regardless of what they’re made of.

However, as relative density increased to 30% and 40%, the performance of the carbon fiber-reinforced PPA (PPA-CF) pulled ahead drastically. At 40% density, as-built PPA-CF gyroids achieved a peak stress outperforming PLA-CF by nearly 50%, sustaining load through a smooth, progressive strain-hardening regime.

Key Takeaway 2: Thermal Annealing is the Real Game-Changer

While adding chopped carbon fibers helps, post-print heat treatment is what truly elevates performance.

When we thermally annealed the PPA-CF gyroids, we saw:

  • Peak stress increase by ~40%
  • Toughness (energy absorption) jump by up to 60%
  • Specific Energy Absorption (SEA) values hitting 24–25 kJ/kg

To put that 24–25 kJ/kg number into perspective, it places these lightweight polymer-composite lattices into the performance envelope typically reserved for metallic TPMS structures (like titanium or steel lattices) — but at a fraction of the weight.

Key Takeaway 3: The Ductility Trade-Off

There is no free lunch in materials science.

While annealing significantly boosts peak load capacity and stiffness, it alters how the lattice fails. FEA simulations showed that fiber reinforcement increases localized stress concentration and plastic strain along load-bearing curves.

As a result, annealed PPA-CF lattices trade off some post-yield ductility. Instead of a perfectly flat, predictable crushing plateau, they experience minor stress drops corresponding to localized internal shear planes.

If your goal is absolute maximum strength and lightweight energy dissipation (think aerospace crash protection or defense applications), annealed PPA-CF is an exceptional choice. If you need a completely smooth, constant crushing force, unreinforced or untreated polymers might still have a role to play.

Final Thoughts

Architected metamaterials aren’t just about tweaking CAD geometry. The interplay between inner topology, fiber orientation, and thermal post-processing dictates how these parts actually behave in the real world.

By coordinating relative density with post-print annealing, chopped-fiber polymer lattices can bridge the gap between heavy metals and compliant plastics.
