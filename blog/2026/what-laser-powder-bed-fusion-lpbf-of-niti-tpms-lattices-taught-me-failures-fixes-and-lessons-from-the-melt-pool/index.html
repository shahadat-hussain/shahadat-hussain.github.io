NiTi is one of those materials that looks magical in theory — shape memory, superelasticity, biocompatibility — and deceptively difficult in practice. Combine it with TPMS lattices, which have huge surface‑area‑to‑volume ratios and complex curvature, and you get a structure that could revolutionize implants, energy absorbers, and adaptive systems… if you can actually print it.

Over the past few years, I spent a lot of time trying to print NiTi TPMS lattices that simply refused to behave. Some warped off the build plate. Others balled up mid‑print. A few looked acceptable on the outside and turned out to be microstructurally chaotic under SEM.

I designed the CAD models, selected the process parameters, and analyzed the microstructures, while a dedicated research engineer operated the EOS M400‑4. These failures — and the process of understanding them — became the foundation of the papers I later published.

This article is the story behind those papers: the problems, the fixes, and what they taught me about NiTi, melt‑pool physics, and the hidden influence of geometry.

1. Delamination: When the Print Peels Itself Off the Plate

One of the earliest and most frustrating failures was delamination (Figure 1). After roughly 90-100 layers, the TPMS structures would detach from the Ti‑alloy build plate. The recoater blade would catch the warped surface, and the print had to be stopped.

Figure 1: Schematic representation of a thin NiTi layer delaminating and peeling away from the build plate substrate due to thermal mismatch and accumulated residual stresses.

The root cause turned out to be simple: NiTi and Ti‑alloy expand and contract differently. The thermal mismatch created residual stresses strong enough to lift the part off the plate.

The fix was surprisingly straightforward: 👉 Use a NiTi build plate instead of Ti‑alloy.

Once we switched, delamination disappeared.

🔹 Lesson: Build‑plate metallurgy matters as much as laser parameters.

2. Balling and Improper Fusion: The Melt Pool Fighting Back

Balling was everywhere — tiny spheres, broken tracks, uneven surfaces (Figure 2). Under SEM, the surfaces looked like miniature meteor fields. Some spheres were 20–40 µm, others exceeded 80 µm depending on density and scan strategy.

Figure 1. Scanning electron micrograph of the surface morphology of LPBF‑fabricated NiTi TPMS lattice showing characteristic imperfections. Spherical balls and uneven surfaces indicate incomplete melting and spattering, while pores and laser melt tracks reveal localized lack‑of‑fusion regions. These features collectively illustrate melt‑pool instability and non‑uniform solidification typical of NiTi under sub‑optimal process parameters. [Ref. 3]

The causes were classic LPBF issues:

  • Insufficient energy density

  • High scan speeds

  • Poor wetting of molten NiTi on the previous layer

  • Rapid heat loss due to complex TPMS curvature

By systematically adjusting laser power, scan speed, and energy density, we eventually stabilized the melt pool. Higher power and slower scanning reduced balling dramatically.

But the real insight was this: 👉 Balling is a melt‑pool stability problem, not just a parameter problem.

🔹 Lesson: If the melt pool is unstable, no parameter chart will save you.

3. Geometry Is a Hidden Process Parameter

Gyroid and primitive lattices behaved very differently, even under identical parameters. Gyroids consistently produced rougher surfaces, more balling, and more uneven melt tracks.

The reason became clear when we compared their surface areas (for the same unit cell size):

  • Primitive (61.1 mm²)

  • Gyroid (121.3 mm²)

The gyroid’s massive surface area drastically increased heat dissipation, which accelerated solidification and destabilized the melt pool.

👉 Geometry shapes the thermal field — and the thermal field shapes the microstructure.

🔹 Lesson: In LPBF of NiTi, geometry is a process parameter.

4. Microstructural Inhomogeneity: A Map of the Thermal History

When we sectioned the samples and examined them under SEM, the microstructures were anything but uniform. We observed:

  • Equiaxed grains

  • Dendritic regions

  • Columnar grains

  • Abrupt transitions within microns

This wasn’t stochastic noise — it was a direct imprint of the local cooling rate and thermal gradient. Primitive lattices showed more mixed grain structures, whereas gyroids tended toward columnar growth due to directional heat flux (Refer Ref. 2).

👉 Microstructure is the most honest record of what the melt pool experienced.

🔹 Lesson: If you want to understand your process, study the grain transitions.

5. Scan Strategy and Density: The Subtle Influencers

In our Materials paper, we explored how scan strategy and relative density affected imperfections. Two surprising findings stood out:

  1. Inclined (45°) scan strategies produced the most severe balling.

  2. Higher relative densities unexpectedly produced larger balling diameters.

These results showed that:

  • Scan vector orientation fundamentally alters heat‑flow direction

  • Density changes the localized thermal mass

👉 Energy density alone cannot predict print quality.

🔹 Lesson: Scan strategy and density quietly shape melt‑pool behavior behind the scenes.

What These Failures Taught Me

Looking back, the most valuable insights didn’t come from the successful prints — they came from the failures.

  1. NiTi demands metallurgical compatibility. Even the build plate matters.

  2. TPMS geometry is a process parameter. Surface area and curvature dictate the life or death of your melt pool.

  3. Defects are clues. Balling, porosity, and grain transitions reveal the true thermal history of your part.

  4. Collaboration accelerates learning. Conversations with researchers saved us months of blind trial‑and‑error.

In LPBF of NiTi, successful prints teach you what worked once. Failed prints teach you how the material actually behaves.
